# KadaVerse Agency Operating Playbook

This is a practical operating plan for a small, founder-led agency. It is built around the work currently presented on the KadaVerse site: client websites and software for catering, construction, education and events, plus the products you build and run yourselves.

## 1. What to sell first

Keep the agency capable of many things, but make the first sales message easy to understand:

> **KadaVerse builds websites and custom software that help growing businesses capture enquiries and run daily operations with less manual work.**

Lead with two offers that match the existing proof:

1. **Enquiry-to-quote system** — a clear website, structured enquiry form, staff inbox and quote or booking flow. Best fit: caterers, consultants, education and other service businesses.
2. **Operations system** — a web dashboard, and mobile app when needed, to replace disconnected spreadsheets, paper and chat groups. Best fit: construction and field teams, hospitality and multi-location businesses.

Offer maintenance, hosting support and growth marketing after a successful build. Do not make every service on the website an equal priority in your first sales conversations. Keep the first project small enough to scope clearly and valuable enough to solve a real business problem. Avoid unpaid custom builds; a short introductory call can be free, while substantial discovery or a prototype should be paid.

## 2. The full client workflow

Use one pipeline for every prospect. Give every lead an owner and a next action with a date.

| Stage | What happens | Exit condition |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Lead captured | Record business, contact, source, likely problem and next step in a simple CRM or board. | A dated follow-up exists. |
| 2. First contact | Send a personal, specific note or request a warm introduction. Follow up politely up to three times over about two weeks. | They reply, decline, or are marked for later. |
| 3. Qualification | Check problem, urgency, decision-maker, budget range, timing and access to required people/data. | A short discovery call is booked, or lead is closed out. |
| 4. Discovery | Understand the current workflow, users, pain, desired outcome, constraints and success measure. | You can explain the problem and proposed first outcome in the client's words. |
| 5. Proposal | Send scope, exclusions, deliverables, milestones, price, assumptions, client responsibilities and support terms. | Client accepts, asks for a revision, or declines. |
| 6. Agreement and deposit | Sign the agreement, collect the agreed initial payment and confirm who can approve work. | No build work starts until these are complete. |
| 7. Kickoff | Collect content, access, brand assets, stakeholder list, decision dates and feedback channel. | Both sides agree on scope, schedule and owners. |
| 8. Design and build | Work in small milestones; show a working preview regularly and record feedback and decisions. | Each milestone is approved against its acceptance criteria. |
| 9. QA and launch | Test agreed user journeys, permissions, mobile layouts, forms, payments/integrations, backups and analytics as relevant. | Client signs off; launch checklist is complete. |
| 10. Handover and support | Transfer source, accounts, documentation and training; explain the included fix period and ongoing support options. | Client confirms receipt and knows how to get help. |
| 11. Review and referral | Ask about the result, request a testimonial and permission for a case study, then ask for one relevant introduction. | Learning and permission are recorded. |

### Qualification checklist

Proceed when you can answer these questions clearly:

- What business problem is costing time, money or missed opportunities?
- Who experiences it and who approves the purchase?
- Why does the client want to solve it now?
- What is the smallest useful first release?
- Is there an agreed budget range and a realistic decision date?
- Can the client provide content, access and feedback on time?
- Can your team deliver it with the skills and capacity available?

If a decision-maker, a real problem, a budget path or timely access is missing, do not rush into a fixed-price proposal. Clarify it or pause the opportunity.

## 3. How to gain clients

### Start with people who can already vouch for you

For the first month, contact past clients, product partners, local business owners, friends in business, accountants, marketing providers, IT consultants, printers and industry associations. Ask for introductions to a specific type of business, not “anyone who needs software.” Request introductions only when appropriate; do not send messages on anyone else's behalf without their approval.

### Build a focused prospect list

Start with 50 businesses in Kannur/Kerala and nearby markets whose public-facing workflow resembles your proven projects. Record the business, decision-maker, likely friction, evidence for that observation, contact channel and follow-up date. Examples to test: caterers handling event enquiries manually, builders coordinating field teams across chats, or consultancies collecting repetitive enquiries.

### Weekly activity targets for the first 90 days

Treat these as learning targets, not promises of sales:

- Add 10 relevant prospects to the list.
- Send 15–25 personal, researched first messages across the week.
- Ask 3–5 people for a specific introduction.
- Follow up on every open conversation on its scheduled date.
- Hold useful discovery calls and send proposals only when the project is qualified.
- Review replies and objections every Friday; change the offer or audience when the evidence says to.

Use the channels where your buyers already respond: warm introductions, direct calls, WhatsApp or email, local business groups and partner referrals. Publish one useful proof item each week—a short case study, workflow teardown or product demonstration. Do not spend heavily on broad ads until you know which offer and audience convert.

### A simple first message

> Hi [Name] — I’m with KadaVerse in Kannur. I saw [specific business detail]. We recently built [relevant kind of system] for [client/project]. If [specific workflow problem] is something you’re dealing with, I can share a few practical options in a 15-minute call. Would that be useful?

Make the observation genuine and the offer relevant. If they are not interested, thank them and stop contacting them unless they invite a later follow-up.

## 4. Discovery, proposal and getting paid

### 30-minute discovery call

1. Ask the client to walk through the current process from start to finish.
2. Find where delays, errors, lost enquiries or duplicate work happen.
3. Ask who uses the system, who decides, and what tools/data already exist.
4. Define the first release and what success will look like.
5. Confirm timing, budget range, risks and decision process.
6. Repeat the problem and proposed next step back to the client.

Do not promise a solution before understanding integrations, legacy systems, data quality and approval needs. If those are unknown, propose a paid discovery phase or technical assessment before fixing the full build price.

### Every proposal should include

- The client's problem and intended outcome.
- Deliverables and acceptance criteria by milestone.
- Explicit exclusions and assumptions.
- Timeline and what the client must provide, with feedback deadlines.
- Price, payment schedule, taxes where applicable, and what pauses work.
- Change-request process and how extra work is priced and scheduled.
- Warranty/fix period, maintenance options and response expectations.
- Ownership, access, confidentiality, data handling and termination terms.

Use milestone payments and keep enough cash to cover delivery costs. For fixed-price work, make the boundaries and acceptance criteria precise. For uncertain work, use a paid assessment or a time-boxed engagement. Never let a verbal request silently expand the agreed scope.

### Scope change rule

When a request falls outside the signed scope, write down: the request, why it is extra, the cost or time impact, and the revised delivery date. Get written approval before starting it. Update the project plan and invoice schedule if needed.

## 5. How to deliver well

### Before kickoff

- Signed agreement and initial payment received.
- One client decision-maker and one feedback channel named.
- Scope, milestone dates, acceptance criteria and dependencies recorded.
- Client content, brand files, test accounts and access requested securely.
- Project folder, issue board, code repository and backup plan created.

### During the project

- Break work into milestones that can be demonstrated.
- Send a concise weekly update: completed, next, blocked, decisions needed, budget/scope changes.
- Demo progress on a staging link; collect feedback in one place.
- Record approvals and decisions in writing.
- Raise risks early, with a recommended action and owner.
- Do not promise a date until dependencies and review time are included.

### Before launch

Use a checklist that fits the project: mobile and browser behavior, user roles, form validation, error states, payment and notification flows, security rules, analytics, search metadata, performance, backups, rollback plan, content approval and client acceptance. Keep production credentials in a password manager, enable multi-factor authentication, use least-privilege access and remove access that is no longer needed.

### At handover

Provide source code, design files, hosting/cloud ownership or transfer, app store access where relevant, environment/setup notes, admin training, user guide, known limitations, backup/restore notes, vendor list, support contact and final invoice status. Put all client-owned accounts in the client's name where possible.

## 6. Running the agency week to week

### Roles—even if one person handles several

- **Sales:** prospect list, introductions, calls, proposals, follow-ups.
- **Delivery lead:** scope, schedule, client updates, risks, approvals.
- **Production:** design, engineering, testing and documentation.
- **Operations:** invoices, collections, bookkeeping, contracts, access, capacity and vendor renewals.

Do not let an urgent client delivery erase sales time. Reserve recurring blocks for outreach and follow-up.

### Weekly rhythm

- **Monday:** review pipeline, cash due, delivery capacity and project risks; set the week's priorities.
- **Daily:** 10-minute check of today's commitments and blockers.
- **Wednesday:** client demos or decision follow-ups; clear stalled approvals.
- **Friday:** send client status updates, reconcile work completed, update the pipeline, review cash and record lessons.
- **Monthly:** review lead sources, win/loss reasons, project margin, overdue invoices, recurring costs, security/access and next month's capacity.

### One source of truth

Use a lightweight CRM for leads, a project board for delivery, shared client folders for approved documents, a bookkeeping/invoicing system for money, and a password manager for credentials. Keep decisions and scope approvals out of scattered personal chats: summarize them in the project record. Avoid adding paid tools until they remove a real bottleneck.

## 7. Money, contracts and business administration

- Separate business and personal finances; reconcile income and expenses monthly.
- Maintain a 13-week cash forecast: opening cash, expected collections, payroll/contractors, taxes, subscriptions and delivery costs.
- Track actual hours and outside costs by project so you can price future work from evidence.
- Set aside money for tax and recurring obligations; do not treat every receipt as profit.
- Use written agreements and invoice records for every project. Keep an approved vendor and renewal list.
- Speak with a qualified Indian accountant and lawyer about the right business structure, tax/GST applicability, invoicing, contracts, intellectual property, privacy/data handling, worker/contractor arrangements and any local registrations. Requirements depend on the agency's facts; this playbook is not legal or tax advice.
- If Udyam/MSME registration may apply, verify the current eligibility and process on the official Government portal; it states that Udyam registration is free and paperless. Use the official portal rather than a paid intermediary. [Udyam Registration](https://udyamregistration.gov.in/default.aspx)
- For GST registration steps, use the official GST portal guidance and have your accountant confirm whether registration applies to your situation. [GST Portal registration guide](https://tutorial.gst.gov.in/userguide/registration/Apply_for_Registration_Normal_Taxpayer.htm)

## 8. What to measure

Review a small scorecard monthly:

| Area | Measure |
|---|---|
| Pipeline | Qualified prospects, discovery calls, proposals and signed projects |
| Acquisition | Reply rate by channel, referral source and reasons for no decision |
| Sales | Proposal win rate, time from first contact to signed agreement, average project value |
| Delivery | Milestones on time, rework, scope changes, client response delays |
| Cash | Invoices due/overdue, collection time, project costs and gross margin |
| Trust | Client feedback, testimonials/case-study permissions, repeat work and referrals |

Use the measures to find the bottleneck. If there are few replies, improve targeting and messages. If calls do not become proposals, qualify better. If proposals do not close, revisit proof, scope, urgency and pricing. If delivery is late or unprofitable, fix scoping, dependencies and project controls before increasing lead volume.

## 9. First 90 days

### Days 1–14: prepare

- Choose the first audience to test and the two offers above.
- Make the four client examples easy to verify: client-approved name, screenshots, problem, solution, testimonial and one measured result if the client can validate it.
- Prepare a one-page proposal, discovery notes template, agreement review checklist, project kickoff checklist and case study template.
- Set up the lead tracker, project board, invoicing/bookkeeping, password manager and business email.
- Ask an accountant/lawyer to review the setup and required obligations.

### Days 15–45: sell consistently

- Build the first 50-prospect list and contact 15–25 relevant businesses each week.
- Ask for 3–5 warm introductions each week.
- Hold discovery calls, document objections and send proposals only to qualified prospects.
- Publish one useful proof item weekly and follow up with every open lead.

### Days 46–90: deliver and compound proof

- Close and deliver one or two appropriately scoped paid projects if pipeline conversion supports it; this is a target, not a guarantee.
- Make client communication, approvals, payment collection and QA consistent.
- Capture a verified before-and-after result, testimonial and permission for each completed project.
- Ask satisfied clients for one introduction and a follow-on support need.
- At day 90, keep the audience/offer combination producing the strongest qualified conversations and revise the rest.

2. What changed after launch?
3. What part of the work was most useful?

5. Who else do you know with a similar problem?

## The rule that keeps the agency healthy

Sell a specific business outcome, agree on the boundaries in writing, show progress regularly, collect payments on schedule, and turn every successful delivery into proof and a referral opportunity.
